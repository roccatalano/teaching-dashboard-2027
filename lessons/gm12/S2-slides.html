  • To be able to construct frequency tables for discrete numerical data.
  • To be able to construct frequency tables for grouped numerical data (discrete and continuous).
  • To be able to construct a histogram from a frequency table for numerical data.

  • To be able to construct a dot plot for numerical data.
  • To be able to construct a stem plot for numerical data.
  • To be able to revise the concept of log10 x.
  • To be able to investigate the effect of the logarithmic scale on the features of a distribution.

Learn · 1C

Frequency tables for numerical data

Discrete, few values

Same as categorical data: one row for every value from the minimum to the maximum.

e.g. number of bedrooms: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5

Many values, or continuous

Group the data into intervals: a grouped frequency table.

  • every value in an interval
  • no overlaps, no gaps
  • about 5 to 15 intervals
  • easy widths: 10, 100, 1000…
  • start each interval on an exact value: 0–49, 50–99
Read it as: 30–<40 means at least 30 but less than 40.

The histogram

The graph of a frequency table for a numerical variable.

  • frequency (count or %) on the vertical axis
  • the variable on the horizontal axis
  • each bar is one interval; its height is the frequency
  • bars touch: the scale is continuous

Learn · 1C

Shape: what to look for

Symmetric

tails off evenly either side of the peak

Positively skewed

long upper tail, to the right

Negatively skewed

long lower tail, to the left

Bimodal

two peaks: often two groups mixed

The tail names the skew. Salaries and house prices: positive. Age at death: negative.
Cambridge General Mathematics Units 3&4, 1C p. 21
Learn · 1C

Centre, spread and outliers

Centre

The middle: the line that splits the area in half.

Spread

Narrow peak: values tightly clustered. Broad peak: widely spread.

Outliers

Values that stand out from the main body: check them, they may be errors.

From a histogram you can only estimate the centre and spread, and only flag possible outliers.

Dot plots and stem plots

Dot plot

A number line with each data point marked by a dot. Equal values stack.

Best for small, discrete data sets.

Stem plot

Each value splits into a stem (leading digits) and a leaf (last digit).

Logs are just powers of 10

number0.010.111010010001 000 000
power of 10\(10^{-2}\)\(10^{-1}\)\(10^{0}\)\(10^{1}\)\(10^{2}\)\(10^{3}\)\(10^{6}\)
log−2−101236

log10 x

If \(\log_{10}x=b\), then \(10^{b}=x\)

Properties

  • number > 1 → log is positive
  • number = 1 → log is 0
  • between 0 and 1 → log is negative
  • 0 or negative → log is undefined
Cambridge General Mathematics Units 3&4, 1E pp. 34–35
Learn · 1E

Why use a log scale?

0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000, 10 000, 100 000, 1 000 000 on an ordinary scale, then on a log scale:

Ordinary scale

Eight numbers bunch at 0; one million sits alone.

Log scale

Evenly spread. Use it when data range over several orders of magnitude. A positively skewed histogram often becomes symmetric.

Worked example 12 · 1E
Using a CAS calculator to find logs
  1. aFind the log of 45, correct to two significant figures.
  2. bFind the number with log equal to 2.7125, correct to the nearest whole number.

a Calculator screen: type log(45).  \(\log 45=1.65\ldots=\) 1.7 (to 2 sig. figs)

b If the log of a number is 2.7125, the number is \(10^{2.7125}\).  \(10^{2.7125}=515.82\ldots=\) 516 (to the nearest whole number)

Undo a log with a power of 10: type 10^2.7125 on the calculator screen.

The histogram shows the distribution of the weights of 27 animal species plotted on a log scale.
  1. aWhat body weight (in kg) is represented by the number 4 on the log scale?
  2. bHow many of these animals have body weights more than 10 000 kg?
  3. cThe weight of a cat is 3.3 kg. Use your calculator to determine the log of its weight correct to two significant figures.
  4. dDetermine the weight (in kg) of the animal with a log(body weight) of 3.4 (the elephant). Write your answer correct to the nearest whole number.

b 10 000 is 4 on the log scale; bars above 4: two animals
